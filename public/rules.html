Field Battles

Rules for resolving a battle between armies in a single sitting.

In one paragraph. Each army is a unit on a hexagon of sixty-one hexes. The sides alternate: one unit activates and takes three actions, then the other side answers. A unit does one of five things — Shoot, Fight, Guard, Rally, Cast — or it moves. Shoot, Fight, Guard and Rally are each a ladder of three rungs, and a unit's grade on that ladder is the rung it takes for free; the rung above is a gamble it may reach for, and the rung above that is out of reach. Cast works differently: every caster's base spell is free, and pushing buys more (section 11). Every act costs one action, and the actions left over buy weight: +2 each, on the roll, on the climb, or on the Defence a Guard sets. Hits deal wounds, one or two at a time, and four wounds destroy a unit. Losing a melee exchange or watching half the army fall costs disorder outright; a wound from a shot, a Blast or a free strike costs it too, unless the target's Fortitude saves against it. A unit whose disorder reaches its Quality is shaken, and one point past it routs. The battle ends when one side has nothing standing, or at dusk after six rounds.

Quick reference

1. What you need

Each army's troop sheet, a d20, and the battle board in the kingdom app: a hexagon of sixty-one hexes, files lettered a to i and ranks numbered 1 to 9, generated from the hex being fought over. Each player commands the armies they lead and any the GM hands them. The GM commands every army of an enemy faction.

A battle happens when armies of hostile factions end up in the same hex. The side that moved in is the attacker; the side already there is the defender. The defender's hex sets the ground (section 10). The battle resolves inside the kingdom's Actions phase, before the next kingdom action.

2. The unit

An army in battle is a unit, and a unit is one of two types: infantry or cavalry. A siege engine is never a unit; it rides with one or holds a hex of its own (section 12). You never invent a number for a unit. Every value comes off the troop sheet, and a card with no sheet takes the level tables for its type.

Level DCs are the standard table: level 1 is DC 15, 3 is 18, 6 is 22, 8 is 24, 12 is 30.

Reflex is the stat that tells troops apart: it spreads 5.6 points within a level where Armour Class spreads 3.2, which is why breaking contact reads off Reflex rather than off Defence. Perception is derived and displayed, but no rule reads it — there is no initiative roll (section 5). Fortitude now reads for one thing: the save a wound from a shot, a Blast or a free strike asks before it disorders anyone (section 8).

A card with no sheet takes the level tables for its type. Infantry: high AC, moderate attack, high Will, moderate Reflex, moderate Fortitude, one hex an action. Cavalry: high AC, high attack, moderate Will, high Reflex, moderate Fortitude, two hexes an action.

Grades

A unit carries a grade of 1, 2 or 3 on each of four ladders. The grade is read off the statblock, never hand-picked. Cast carries no grade of its own — a tradition caps how far a caster may push instead (section 11).

LadderGrade comes from
Shoot1 for every troop with an ordinary Salvo; a covering-fire tactic grants 3 outright

An importer reads melee drill, shields, formation, magic ward, mounted and no retreat straight off the recurring action names on a statblock. A trained tactic, where a card carries one, raises one grade to 3 and does nothing else: covering fire raises Shoot; a reactive attack, dirty fighting or a feint raises Fight; raised shields or a shield block raises Guard; defending allies or battlefield medicine raises Rally.

Armour Class and attack DC carry no grade information

Across all 162 published troops, Armour Class spreads 3.2 points within a level and attack DC 2.6 — at level 12 every troop shares one attack DC. Both are functions of level, so neither says anything about a troop's character. Will spreads 5.1 and Speed falls across seven bands, so those two are read numerically, and the recurring action names carry the rest.

Quality

A unit's Quality is the disorder it absorbs before it is shaken, and it too comes off the Will save: 2 below the low band, 3 at low, 4 at moderate, 5 at high, 6 at extreme. Line Infantry has Quality 3; Kobold Warriors, better disciplined for their level, have 4; Troll Marauders, who are not disciplined at all, have 2 and are shaken by two bad exchanges.

3. The battlefield

The battlefield is a hexagon of sixty-one hexes, in rows of 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 8, 7, 6 and 5. Files run a to i and ranks 1 to 9, from the attacker's edge to the defender's; e5 is the centre. Name a hex by file and rank: c3, e7. Every unit stands on one hex, and a hex holds at most one unit.

Hexes have no diagonal. Every one of a hex's six neighbours is adjacent, at distance 1, and the distance between two units is the number of steps from one hex to the other. Distance sets the band a shot falls in:

DistanceBandNote
1EngagedFight. No shooting either way.
2Short
3–4Medium
5–6Long
7–8ExtremeNo troop's own Salvo is anchored here — only a siege engine's effective range starts this far out
9 or moreBeyondNever occurs on this board: extreme's own ceiling is already the farthest two hexes can be

Which rung reaches a given band depends on the shooter, not the band. Fire is free at a weapon's own effective range (its Reach, section 2); Aim swings one band off it and Snipe two, either direction, whichever way the target actually is (section 6). A short-reach troop's Snipe can still reach as far as long, but no further — extreme takes a medium or long effective range pushing in, or a siege engine's own reach, which starts there for free.

Two enemy units on adjacent hexes are engaged, unless a cliff lies on the edge between them. A unit engaged with two or more enemies is outflanked and takes −2 to Defence. An engaged unit cannot stride away and cannot shoot; it fights, guards, rallies, casts, or withdraws (section 7).

Hexes carry terrain and height; edges carry walls and cliffs (section 10).

4. Setting up

Setup runs in five stages, each on its own screen in the app.

Stage 1: the battlefield

Pick the hex's terrain (plains, forest, hills, mountains, swamp or desert), add a feature if the hex has one (a river or a lakeside) and a construction if it holds one (a fort of tier 0 to 3), then roll a seed. The generator lays the board out, and the same seed always gives the same board. Reroll until it looks like the hex.

Terrain is a tendency, not a rule: a plains board can carry a copse and a hills board a marsh, never many. Hills and desert raise a low ridge, mountains a high one with peaks. A river runs across ranks 4 to 6, the band neither side deploys on, and carries one or two fords; a lake hugs one flank with a marshy shore. Water never lands on a deployment rank, so both sides can always deploy.

Hex terrainWhat the generator lays down

ForestEight to eleven woods of three to six hexes
HillsA low ridge, one to three copses
MountainsA high ridge with one or two peaks, four to seven woods

Stage 2: paint

The GM adjusts the board by hand: set any hex's terrain, set its height to 0, 1 or 2, put a wall on any edge, or clear a hex. This is where a hazard from the adventure goes — the bridge, the burning barn, the ravine.

Stages 3 and 4: the two forces

Build each side from the troop library, the force generator or a custom card, attach siege engines to the units that bring them, then put every unit on a hex. The attacker deploys anywhere on ranks 1 to 3, the defender anywhere on ranks 7 to 9, never on water. A unit with an ambush tactic may deploy one rank further in. Both sides need at least one unit, and every unit needs a hex. Note each unit's wounds and disorder from its sheet.

Three ranks lie between the lines. A unit on rank 3 reaches contact in its first activation; a unit held back on rank 1 takes two. The depth of your deployment is your first decision.

Every player commands a unit. When the players' side brings fewer armies than players, split armies before deployment: one army becomes two or more units of the same card, each with its own hex, wounds and disorder. Each half is a full unit, a separate target for outflanking and a separate candidate for routing; a player who commands one may lose it. After the battle the halves rejoin: wounds take the average rounded up, disorder the higher. The GM may also hand an allied faction's unit to a player for the battle.

The opposing force. The GM picks the enemy from the library or lets the generator build one. The generator reads the other side and answers it: the same number of units give or take one, every unit within three levels of the other side's average, and the total level within a tenth of the other side's. At most one unit brings a siege engine. An attacker facing walls brings one three times in four, and it may be a ram; anyone else brings artillery one time in five. Add to the generated force or take from it.

Stage 5: begin

The attacker's side activates first. There is no initiative roll.

5. The round

A round is an hour of battle, and every unit acts once in it. The sides alternate: after each activation, the side with more units still to act goes next, and on a tie the side that did not act last. You choose which of your own units takes each of your turns.

An activation is three actions. A unit attacks once per activation — one Fight, one Shoot, or one Blast. Further actions buy weight on what it is already doing, never a second attack.

When every unit has acted, the round ends:

  1. Each side that has lost half its starting units to death or rout takes it once, that round only: every unit of that side still standing gains 1 disorder.
  2. An emplaced engine with only the enemy beside it changes hands. Every engine reloads.
  3. If either side has no unit still standing, the battle ends. If this was the sixth round, dusk ends it.

Alternating activation is what keeps a round readable: no side ever takes its whole army's worth of actions while the other watches, and a side that outnumbers the other spends its surplus at the end of the round rather than the start.

6. Ladders, grades and weight

Four things a unit can do this way, and each is a ladder of three rungs. The rung is what happens; the grade is how high the unit climbs for free. A fifth, Cast, works differently — no grade, a tradition-capped tree instead of a rung, and its own push (section 11).

The menu is filtered by where the unit stands, so it is never long. In contact: Fight and Guard. In the open: Shoot and Guard. A shaken unit is offered Rally alone, and a routed one nothing at all. Rally is otherwise always offered, and a caster adds a row for each of the six trees its tradition grants. An attacker beside a wall may Fight the wall even with nobody defending it.

Every act costs one action, whatever rung it lands on. Any actions left in the activation buy one of two different things, and they are not the same currency. Reach is a free gamble that decides which rung fires. Weight is a guaranteed bonus, paid for in actions, that only ever changes a number. A unit can reach, weight, both, or neither — they draw on the same pool of spare actions but land on different things.

Reach: climbing above your grade

A unit takes any rung at or below its grade with no roll at all. One rung above costs nothing but a d20; two rungs above is out of reach however many actions the unit has left.

A reach costs nothing but the risk. The act goes ahead either way, so the question is never whether to act but how high to aim.

Weight: spending actions for a number

Weight is the other thing a spare action buys: +2, guaranteed, no roll, stacked on the same act as many times as the unit has actions left. It can land in four places:

Landing spotWhat it does
RollThe act's own roll: the melee, the shot, the Rally check, the casting, the escape. Guard has no roll of its own.
PushThe reach check, when the unit is also climbing above its grade — weight can back a gamble as easily as a sure thing.
DefenceGuard only. A Guard sets a number outright rather than rolling: the actions committed to it × 2, counting the first. Brace alone is +2 Defence, two actions make it +4, three make it +6.
DistanceWithdraw only. Each action buys another Speed's worth of ground to run (section 7).

So a unit with three actions might Strike and put both spare actions on the roll, at +4; or reach for Press with one action on the climb and one on the melee, splitting the two paths; or dig in at +6 Defence and dare the field to come to it. The rung carries the effect and the actions carry the numbers — that separation is what keeps a weak unit's grade-1 act worth taking at all.

One attack an activation. Fight, Shoot and a Blast are the same slot. A second attack was the thing that broke the pacing, so the spare actions buy weight instead. Everything else — Guard, Rally, the other spells, moving — may be repeated.

7. Moving and breaking away

Move

One Move action buys the unit's hexes (the row above), and a hex costs that budget by its terrain: 1 for open ground or a settlement, 2 for forest or shallows, 3 for swamp, plus 1 for each level of height climbed. Water cannot be entered at all. So a 1-hex-an-action infantry troop spends two actions to enter forest and three to enter swamp, and a 2-hex-an-action cavalry troop spends one and two.

Movement banks across the whole activation rather than lapsing at the end of each Move, so a 2-hex troop that steps one open hex keeps the leftover hex for the next step. A unit may not enter an occupied hex, cross a standing wall, or cross a cliff. A breach in a wall is an ordinary crossing.

A flier pays 1 a hex whatever the ground and crosses walls and cliffs. Flight buys no extra distance: how far a unit goes is still its Speed.

Charge

A charge is the movement it takes plus one action for the melee, and the melee is an ordinary Fight rung with all the same dials. It is one act, so it spends the activation's attack.

Push

A push reaches for ground beyond every action the unit has left — one action's worth further, and no more. Roll d20 + Will, less disorder, +2 if the unit is mounted or trained to charge, and +2 for every action beyond the first, against your own level DC. A push always spends every action left, win or lose.

Withdraw

Withdrawing is the only way out of contact, and it is the only way a shaken or routed unit moves at all — neither may Stride. It costs one action, and it is not a ladder: it is one Escape check against each enemy holding you — d20 + Reflex, less disorder, against that enemy's attack DC, which is its Strike plus 10. An enemy with no melee attack holds nobody.

Having broken away, the unit moves one hex clear of everything it was engaged with, and each action put on the distance dial buys another Speed's worth of ground. A unit with nowhere clear to go is offered the rest rather than being stuck. A troop that gives no retreat follows: one free move of its own Speed to re-establish contact, dealing no damage. Outrunning it is the only way clear.

8. Wounds

Damage is never rolled. A hit deals one wound, a critical hit two, and each unit holds four. The level difference between attacker and target does the work damage dice do elsewhere: a level-6 troop striking a level-2 troop hits on about 7 and criticals from 17, and the reverse hits only from 17 and cannot critical without help.

A unit that enters battle already hurt starts with wounds: 0 above three-quarters of its hit points, 1 above half, 2 above a quarter, 3 with any left. After the battle they are written back the same way.

The melee exchange

A Fight is an exchange, not a blow. The attacker rolls d20 + Strike against the target's Defence; then the target, if it still stands and still has a melee attack, strikes back the same way. Critical success deals 2 wounds, success 1, failure nothing. A critical failure leaves the striker exposed: −2 Defence until it next activates.

The wounds of an exchange do not disorder anybody. The exchange itself is the morale event: whichever side took more wounds gains 1 disorder, and if the attacker climbed to Press, the loser takes 2. An exchange that comes out even costs nobody anything.

Overrun changes what happens after: if the target is destroyed or routed by the exchange and its hex is clear, the attacker takes the ground.

Shooting

A shot is one roll: d20 + Volley against the target's Defence, with the same two wounds on a critical and one on a hit. A wound from a shot, a Blast or a free strike sends the target to a Fortitude save — d20 + Fortitude, less disorder, against the attacking creature's level DC — and only a failure costs it 1 disorder. A melee exchange asks no such save: its wounds were never the morale event to begin with (above).

The rung sets how far off the shooter's own effective range the shot reaches. Fire is that range itself, free; Aim swings one band off it and Snipe two, in whichever direction the target actually is — nearer or farther. Weight bought with a spare action goes on the roll or on that reach check, the player's choice; nothing about the rung itself touches cover. Shooting is not possible while engaged, in either direction.

Defence

Every attack rolls against the target's Defence, and Defence is built the same way each time.

A unit that has dug in caps every hit it takes at one wound, so a critical against it lands as an ordinary one.

9. Disorder and rout

Morale is one number: disorder. Each point is −1 to everything the unit rolls and −1 to its Defence. Morale runs one point past Quality, and the two bands above it are different things.

When disorder reaches the unit's Quality (section 2) the unit is shaken: it may do nothing but Rally or withdraw. It holds its hex if it wants to, it still counts as standing, and Rally is a real way back — a shaken unit that clears a point is a unit again.

One point past Quality and it is routed: nothing but the withdrawal, it runs for its own edge, and it leaves the field when it reaches it. Disorder stops there; a routed unit cannot be driven further. Its own Rally is gone, so the only way back is an adjacent ally's Rally or an Inspire within 2 — which is what makes the troop beside a breaking one worth keeping there.

Rally

Every unit that is not routed may Rally, whatever else its situation allows: in contact or in the open, in good order or shaken. A shaken unit has Rally and the withdrawal and nothing else, which is what makes the act its way back. The rout is the only thing that takes it away, and then only the unit's own — an ally's Rally still reaches it.

Rally is the only thing that clears disorder. Roll d20 + Will, less disorder, against the rout DC: the level DC of the highest-level enemy within short range, or, when no enemy is that close, of the highest-level enemy on the field. Rallying under a dragon's eye is harder than rallying beside a levy.

The roll says how much clears; the rung says who else it reaches. Every rung lets an ally take heart — +2 on its attacks until it has acted — and from Rally upward the ally clears a point as well. This is why a unit with nothing to clear still has a use for the act: a levy's Steady is a poor self-rally and a real gift to the troop beside it.

10. Ground, height and walls

Three layers shape the field. Terrain fills a hex and changes what checks cost; height decides who has the advantage; barriers sit on edges and decide where units may go. Forest hides, swamp slows, water blocks, height commands, walls hold.

Height

Every hex stands at height 0, 1 or 2. Count the difference between the two hexes:

  • Attacking upward, in melee or with a shot, costs −1 for each level of difference.
  • Shooting downward counts the range one band closer, and the target's forest cover does not apply.
  • Climbing costs 1 a level on top of the terrain.
  • An edge with a difference of 2 is a cliff: nobody crosses it, and nobody is engaged across it.

Walls

A wall sits on the edge between two hexes. Nobody crosses it until it is breached, but units on either side are engaged across it and shots cross it freely. An attacker striking across a standing wall takes −2. A defender standing beside a wall of its own is garrisoned and shoots at +1.

Only the attacker attacks a wall, and each segment has wound boxes equal to its tier plus one. Fight a segment on an edge of your own hex, or bombard one within your rung's band with artillery. The DC is 10 + the wall's tier + the level of the highest defender still standing; a hit takes one box, a critical two, and a segment with no boxes left is breached and gone. A ram rolls at +2 and is good for nothing else. Walls are repaired through fortification maintenance, never during a battle.

A fort is a block of settlement hexes against the defender's edge, walled on its front and flanks with the segments its tier allows: four edges at tier 0, up to seven at tier 3. A short budget leaves a gate open on one flank. Whatever the fortification gives the garrison in Defence and saves is already on its sheet.

11. Magic

A unit whose statblock names a spellcasting entry is a caster, and every caster belongs to one of magic's four traditions: arcane, divine, occult, primal. A tradition does not carry every kind of magic — see Tradition, below.

Casting

A caster carries two more numbers on its sheet, alongside Battle DC and Salvo DC: spell attack, what the caster itself rolls, and spell DC, what a target must beat to resist it. Neither is Will or the level-DC table doing double duty — they're the caster's own imported numbers, the same way Strike and Battle DC already are.

The cast itself is free: no roll, at whichever tree's own base range (below), resolving at Tier 1 of whichever tree you choose. Pushing is what introduces risk. Reaching for extended range, a longer duration, or a stronger effect — never more than one of the three on a single cast — commits you to a cast roll: d20 + spell attack, less disorder, against your own level DC, +2 when the tier reached for is the third — the same reach formula every other ladder uses, with spell attack standing in for Will. Crit success climbs one tier further than you reached for, capped at the third; success gets you what you reached for; failure falls back to the free base and the cast still happens, the same as every other ladder's reach; critical failure falls back and costs you 1 disorder.

Once the cast lands, an enemy-targeted tree — Blast or Controlling — needs a second, separate effect roll to see how well it bites, decoupled from the cast roll that got it there. Neither is the caster rolling an attack: the target resists instead, the same shape as an Escape check resists a holder's own attack DC. Blast asks for Reflex, Controlling for Will — the target rolls d20 + that save, less disorder, against the caster's own spell DC. Blast is still the activation's one attack, the same slot a Fight or a Shot uses. An ally-targeted tree — Healing, or the Offense, Defense or Movement buff — has nothing to resist, so it simply applies once the cast lands, no second roll.

A caster also draws on a second pool ordinary actions can't touch: level ÷ 5, rounded down, spendable only on a Cast push — 0 below level 5, rising to 4 at level 20. It refreshes every activation, exactly as the ordinary three actions do. Cast carries no grade of its own: there is no second number gating which tiers are free, the way grade still does for Shoot, Fight, Guard and Rally. The pool is the one mechanic deciding how far a push goes, stacked on top of whatever a caster's ordinary three actions also commit to the same cast.

The six trees

A tier is a different kind of thing, not a bigger number on the same thing — the same rule every rung already follows. Cast · range and Cast · duration aren't trees a tradition gates; every caster pushes them the same way regardless of tradition. Pushing either where the tree it would improve has nothing to use it — a duration push on a Blast still at Tier 1, either push on an ally-targeted tree once its own effect roll doesn't exist to bonus, or a second band of range on a Blast already pushed one band from long to extreme, the board's own ceiling — is legal and simply buys nothing, the same as a dial with nowhere to land elsewhere in the system.

12. Siege engines

An engine is attached to a unit and rides with it, or emplaced on a hex of its own. An emplacement is worked by whichever friendly unit stands in or beside it, and it needs no crew of its own; leave it alone and it is abandoned where it stands.

A loaded artillery piece replaces its unit's shooting profile, effective range and all: the unit shoots with the engine's launch bonus, from the engine's own Reach rather than its own, and always at grade 3 — a gun crew works its whole engineered spread without the gamble an ordinary troop's own reach check carries. An engine's Reach may be extreme, which no troop's own Salvo ever is; that is what siege equipment is for. Shooting into the extreme band costs an ordinary troop's own volley −2, which artillery ignores, and no engine may fire while its crew is engaged, because nobody shoots in contact. A ram fights walls at +2 and does nothing else. Each engine fires once a round, whoever works it; where two friendly units both stand beside an emplacement, the crew is the first in deployment order.

An emplaced engine never moves, so an emplaced ram can only ever batter a wall on an edge of the hex it deployed in. A ram is normally attached.

A unit that is destroyed or routs abandons its engines where it stands. An emplacement left with only the enemy beside it changes hands at the end of the round — the engine is taken by standing on it, not by winning a fight over it — and an abandoned engine beside an enemy still standing at the end of the battle is captured.

13. Ending the battle

The battle ends at the end of a round: when a side has no unit still standing, or after the sixth round, whichever comes first. A unit is standing when it is neither destroyed nor routed nor gone from the field; a shaken unit still counts, so a side reduced to shaken units has not yet lost.

Dusk

If both sides still stand after six rounds, both withdraw, keep their wounds and disorder, and the hex stays contested.

The kingdom's checks

What a battle's outcome buys the kingdom — Fame, Unrest, recovery — is not these rules' concern. A battle ends at wounds, disorder, destroyed and captured; what a wider campaign does with that result is a kingdom action of its own.

Back on the kingdom map

Wounds become hit points on the same four thresholds (section 8). Disorder becomes the army's demoralized value, keeping whichever is higher; an army at demoralized 3 disbands at upkeep unless it is rallied or supported. Split armies rejoin (section 4). Destroyed units disband; a captured engine changes owner and an abandoned one is lost. The loser's surviving units fall back one hex toward their nearest friendly settlement.

14. Fighting against the odds

There are no points. Four things decide an uneven fight:

  • Level. Two levels of difference is noticeable; five is decisive. Level sets the DC of your own reaches too, so a high-level unit climbs its ladders no more easily than a low-level one — it simply starts higher.
  • Quality. A troop with a poor Will save is shaken at 2 disorder, which is two lost exchanges, and routed at 3. Trolls hit harder than almost anything at their level and break faster than a militia.
  • Numbers. Outflanking pays only when it reaches a second side of the enemy, and a ridge or a river decides where that second side is. Three lesser units grind down one greater one through disorder, not through wounds.
  • Ground and time. Forest, high ground and walls favour the defender. Six rounds and the half-army check reward a decisive attacker and punish a hesitant one.

A side whose total levels are less than half the enemy's should hold ground or refuse battle.
